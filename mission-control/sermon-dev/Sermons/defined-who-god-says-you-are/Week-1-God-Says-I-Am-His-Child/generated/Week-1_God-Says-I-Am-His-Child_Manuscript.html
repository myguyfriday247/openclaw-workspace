God Says I Am His Child

Series: Defined — Who God Says You Are | Week 1 | April 12, 2026
Romans 8:1–17 | ReImage Church — Pastor Chris

Have you ever had someone try to give you something — a real gift, no strings — and you just couldn't take it?

Like, somebody at a restaurant says, "Hey, I got your meal." And before they even finish the sentence, you're reaching for your wallet. "No, no, no — let me at least get the tip." And they're like, "I said I got it." And you're like, "You sure? I can Venmo you." They are literally handing you a gift and you are negotiating your way into paying for it.

Come on. We all do this.

And here's the thing — we don't just do it at restaurants. We do it with God.

God says, "You're mine. You're in. You're my child." And we say, "Okay, but let me earn it first. Let me prove I'm worth it. Let me pray enough and read my Bible enough and serve enough and be good enough — and then maybe I'll believe it."

I want to be honest with you this morning. I've been a Christian for a long time. My dad was a pastor. I grew up in the church. I've been in ministry for decades. And there are still weeks — real weeks, recent weeks — where I wake up and the first thought in my head is, "Is God pleased with me today?" And that question sits on my chest like a weight. I know better. I know the theology. I can teach it. I've preached it to you. But knowing it and living in it are two different things.

How many of you have had that thought? Come on, don't be shy. I've had it this week.

That voice — the one that says you have to keep earning it, keep proving it, keep performing — that's what I call the orphan voice. And if you've heard it, this message is for you.

Because here's what God wants you to know today. You're not an orphan trying to earn a home. You're a child who's already home.

This is Week 1 of a new series called "Defined" — four weeks about who God says you are. Not who you think you are. Not who the world says you are. Who God says you are. And it starts here: you are His child.

Let me pray for us, and then we'll get into the text.

Father, we thank you that you've called us your children. Open our eyes to see who you say we are. Remove the lies we've believed about ourselves and replace them with your truth. We want to hear from you today. In Jesus' name, amen.

All right. I want to start with something that changed everything for me when I finally understood it. Because for a long time, I had the gospel partly right and partly sideways. And the part I had sideways was costing me more than I realized.

For years I thought forgiveness was the headline. The main event. I thought the gospel was basically: God forgives your sins, and if you hold it together, you get to go to heaven. And that is true — but it's not the whole truth. It's not even the best part.

Because here's what happens when forgiveness is your primary understanding of the Christian life. You wake up every day trying not to sin. You try to pray enough. You try to read your Bible enough. And every time you fail — and you will — you feel like you lost something. Like the balance went down. Like God is looking at you with his arms crossed. Like you have to earn back what you lost.

But that is not the good news.

God doesn't just save us from sin. He brings us into His family.

Now, I know what some of you are thinking. "Pastor Chris, are you saying forgiveness isn't enough?" No. I'm saying forgiveness and adoption are doing two different things. Forgiveness deals with guilt. Adoption deals with identity. Most of us got stuck on the first one and never fully received the second.

Think about it this way. If somebody owed you money and you said, "We're even. I forgive the debt" — the debt is gone. That's powerful. But the person walks away. The debt is clear. There's no relationship.

But when you adopt someone, you do something completely different. You don't just clear a record. You give them your name. Your inheritance. Your family. Your future. You say, "You belong here now. This is your home. You are mine."

Forgiveness clears the debt. Adoption gives you the name.

And that's what God did. God didn't just say "we're even." He said "you're family."

Now let me walk you through what Paul is doing in Romans 8, because he doesn't just drop the adoption line and move on. He builds an argument. And I don't want us to skip to the punchline — I want us to see how he gets there.

He starts in verse 1. This is the foundation. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."

No condemnation. Before Paul says anything about adoption, before he says "Abba, Father" — he starts here. The verdict is already in. The case is closed. If you are in Christ, you are not condemned. Period.

Then he tells us why. Verses 2 through 4 — the law of the Spirit of life has set you free from the law of sin and death. What the law couldn't do — because we were too weak to keep it — God did. He sent His own Son. And the righteous requirement of the law was fulfilled in us — not through our effort, but through the Spirit.

Not through your effort. Through the Spirit.

Then he keeps building. Verses 9 through 11. If the Spirit of God lives in you, you belong to Christ. That's not a question mark — that's a period. The Spirit living in you is the proof. You don't have to wonder "Am I in?" The Holy Spirit living inside of you is God's way of saying, "You're in."

And that leads Paul to the climax. Verses 14 through 16. "For all who are led by the Spirit of God are sons of God. For you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!' The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God."

Sons. Children. Not employees. Not contractors. Not interns trying to prove they deserve a full-time offer. Children. That's the word. And Paul chose it on purpose.

The Greek word he uses is huiothesia. It literally means "son-placement." God placed you in the family. He put you there. On purpose. He didn't accidentally leave the door open and you wandered in. He chose you. He placed you. He said, "That one's mine."

And that word — "Abba." That's not "Oh great and powerful God in the heavens." That's not formal religious language. That's the word a little kid uses when they run to their dad. "Daddy." That's the relationship God wants with you. Not distant. Not transactional. Intimate. Close. Personal.

And verse 17 — if children, then heirs. Heirs of God. Joint heirs with Christ. You don't just get into the family — you get the inheritance. Everything that belongs to Christ belongs to you.

Now, here's a detail that matters. In the Roman world — and Paul is writing to Romans, so this lands — adoption was an absolute legal act. The adopted child had every right of a biological child. They couldn't be disinherited. They couldn't be kicked out. They couldn't be un-adopted. Once you were in, you were in. Permanently.

That was true in the Roman world. It's even more true in the Kingdom of God.

And when did God decide to adopt you?

Was it when you walked down an aisle? When you said a prayer? When you got baptized?

No. Listen to this. Ephesians 1:4–5: "He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world... He predestined us for adoption to Himself as sons, through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of His will."

Before the foundation of the world. Before you were born. Before you could do anything good or bad. He chose you.

And He didn't choose you because He foresaw that you'd be a great Christian. He didn't look down the corridor of time and say, "She's going to get it right. I'll pick her." He chose you because He wanted to. Because that's who He is.

And notice what that verse says — "through Jesus Christ." He could choose you because He already knew what it would cost. He knew it would cost His Son. And He sent Him anyway. The adoption papers were signed in blood. His blood. Before you ever asked.

That's not because of your worth. That's because of His love.

One more verse and I want to make sure this lands. First John 3:1. I love this because you can hear the astonishment in John's voice:

"See what kind of love the Father has given to us, that we should be called children of God; and so we are."

Did you catch those last three words? "And so we are." John writes the sentence — "that we should be called children of God" — and then he can't help himself. He adds three more words. "And so we are." Like he's saying, "I know this sounds too good. I know you're thinking there must be a catch. But there isn't. And so we are."

You are God's child. And so you are.

God didn't just clear your record. He gave you His name. You didn't earn a seat at the table — your name was already on it.

So what does this mean for us? If God didn't just forgive you but adopted you — if He gave you His name, His inheritance, His family — then your identity isn't something you have to build. It's something you received. You didn't produce it. You can't lose it. It was given to you before you could do anything to earn it or mess it up.

The next time you feel like you're on the outside looking in — like you have to earn your way back into God's good graces — you can stop. The door is already open. You're already in. You have a name. You have a Father. You have a home.

Now, I want to give you a few things to sit with this week — not assignments, just starting points. The Holy Spirit might use one of these. He might have something completely different. But listen — and let Him direct your step.

One thought: try praying using the word "Father" this week. Not "God." Not "Lord." Just "Father." And notice what it does. Notice how the intimacy of that word changes the way you talk to Him. He's not a distant authority keeping score. He's your Dad. Maybe that's what God is inviting you into this week — just the intimacy of that word.

Another thought: the next time you fail — and you will — try saying this out loud: "I'm still His child." Fight the orphan voice with the truth. You didn't lose your adoption because you had a bad day. His love didn't fluctuate. The verdict is still in. Maybe God wants to use that moment of failure to remind you who you are.

Or maybe He's asking something else entirely. Maybe He wants you to sit with 1 John 3:1 and let those three words wash over you. "And so we are." I don't know what He's saying to you. But I know He's saying something. Listen.

So here's the question I know some of you are already asking — because I'd be asking it too. "Pastor Chris, if all of that is true — if I'm adopted, if I'm chosen, if the verdict is in — then why do I still feel like I have to earn it? Why do I feel like I'm one bad week away from God being done with me?"

Good question. That's exactly what we need to talk about. Because that feeling has a name. And a lot of us are living in it.

I call it the orphan mindset. And here's what it looks like.

An orphan always feels like they have to prove themselves. A child knows they belong.

The orphan thinks: "If I don't perform, I won't be loved." The orphan thinks: "My worth is based on what I do." The orphan runs the math every single day — did I pray enough? Did I read my Bible? Did I serve? Did I give? Did I do enough? — and the answer is always the same. Not quite. Never enough. Almost. And so they keep striving. Keep performing. Keep running on the treadmill. And they are exhausted.

How many of you know that is exhausting?

And I'm not talking about people who don't know Jesus. I'm talking about people who do. People who are saved. People who have been in church for years. People in this room right now. They love God — but they live like orphans. They perform for acceptance. They base their worth on how well they did this week. And they never feel like they've done enough.

Maybe you've felt this: you approach God with fear instead of confidence. Prayer doesn't feel like talking to your dad — it feels like reporting to a boss. You're not sure He's happy with you. You know the verse says He loves you, but you don't feel it. And every time you mess up, that voice doesn't just whisper — it shouts: "See? You're not good enough. You never will be."

That's the orphan mindset. And it is killing us.

Most of you know the story of the prodigal son. The younger son leaves, blows his inheritance, comes crawling back. The father runs to him, embraces him, throws a feast. Beautiful picture of grace.

But there's another son in that story. And he's the one I want to talk about.

The older brother. He never left home. He stayed. He served his father faithfully for years. Did everything right. Good kid. Responsible kid. Showed up every day and did the work.

And then the younger son comes home, and the father throws a party. And the older brother is furious. Won't even go inside. And when his father comes out to find him, listen to what he says. Luke 15:29 — "Look, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed your command, yet you never gave me a young goat, that I might celebrate with my friends."

Do you hear that word? Served. He didn't say "I've been your son for all these years." He said "I've been serving you." Slaving. Working. Earning. Performing.

He was in the house the whole time — but he never felt like a son. He felt like a hired hand. He lived under his father's roof, ate at his father's table, worked in his father's fields — and he still felt like an outsider. He was home, but he was living like an orphan.

I think a lot of us are the older brother.

We've been in the church a long time. We show up. We serve. We give. We do the things. And deep down, we're keeping score — wondering when it's going to be enough. Wondering when we'll finally feel like we belong.

But here's what the father says to the older brother — and don't miss this. Verse 31: "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours."

Son. You are always with me. All that is mine is yours.

He was a son the whole time. He just didn't know it. He had the inheritance the whole time. He just never received it. He was home the whole time. He just never felt like he belonged.

That might be some of you today.

And there's a reason this is so hard to shake. Psychologists call it "contingent self-worth" — when your sense of value depends on meeting conditions. On performing. On being good enough. The research is clear: people who live like that experience more anxiety, more shame, more fear of failure. Their identity is always on the line.

And a lot of Christians live exactly like that. Saved — and still performing every day for a Father who already said yes.

The orphan asks, "Am I enough?" The child says, "My Father is."

Remember what I said at the beginning about the restaurant — somebody trying to pay you back for a free meal? Let me escalate that.

Imagine someone comes to you and says, "I want to give you a million dollars. No strings. It's yours. It's a gift."

And you take it. And it's amazing. But then — the next morning — you show up at their house. And you start cleaning. You're mopping their floors. Mowing their lawn. Washing their car. And they come outside and look at you like, "What are you doing?"

"Well, I just want to earn it. I want to prove I deserve it."

And they're looking at you like — "I gave you a million dollars so you'd never have to clean a house again. Not mine. Not yours. Not anybody's. Why are you trying to pay me back for something I gave you for free?"

That's us with God. He gives us everything — adoption, inheritance, the Holy Spirit, eternal life — and we keep showing up to clean the house. We keep trying to pay back a gift. And God is saying, "You're my child. Come inside. Sit down. You're home. Stop trying to pay for what's already yours."

You can't earn what was already given. That's what makes it grace.

So which voice are you living under?

Are you living under the orphan voice — always performing, always proving, always wondering if you've done enough? Or are you living under the Spirit's voice — the one that says no condemnation, you belong, you're in, you're mine?

When do you feel like you have to prove yourself to God? Is it when you mess up? When you skip your quiet time? When that shame starts creeping in? When you compare yourself to someone who seems like they've got it way more together than you do? Is it Sunday morning when you walk in here wondering if anyone can tell you didn't read your Bible all week?

That's the orphan voice. And it's lying to you. The Spirit is saying something different. "There is therefore now no condemnation." "The Spirit Himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God." The orphan voice says "prove it." The Spirit says "it's already proven."

You've got to learn to fight it. Not with more performance — with truth.

A couple things to sit with this week. You know the rhythm — starting points, not assignments. Listen for what God is actually saying.

Pay attention to when the orphan voice shows up. Just notice it. When does the anxiety hit? When do you feel like God is disappointed? Don't try to fix it yet — just name it. "That's the orphan voice." You can't fight what you can't see. Sometimes just naming it takes away half its power.

And write down three truths about who you are in Christ. "I am loved. I am chosen. I am His child. I belong. I am not condemned." Put them where the orphan voice is loudest — your mirror, your lock screen, your dashboard. When it talks, replace it with the truth. Out loud if you have to.

Or maybe God is asking you to sit with the older brother's story and ask yourself: "Where am I keeping score?" Listen. He knows what you need.

Now I know where some of you are going. "Okay, Pastor Chris. If I'm already adopted. If I don't have to earn it. If the verdict is in — then what do I do? Do I just coast? Is it all grace and no obedience?"

No. And this is the part that changes how you actually live.

When you know whose you are, you know who you are. And that changes everything about how you live.

Here's what most of us have been doing. We've been trying to change our behavior so we can earn an identity. Pray more so God loves me more. Serve more so I feel like I belong. Read my Bible more so I can call myself a real Christian. We're working from the outside in. Behavior first, identity second.

But that's backwards. That's behavior modification. And the result is always the same — guilt and exhaustion. You can never do enough. The bar keeps moving. You're always falling short of a standard that was never the point.

Identity transformation works the other way around. It starts with who you are — and lets that shape what you do. It starts with the truth — "I am God's child. I belong. I am not condemned" — and everything else flows from there. When you know who you are, you don't have to manufacture obedience. It comes from somewhere deeper. It comes from belonging.

Your obedience doesn't create your identity. Your identity creates your obedience.

That's the shift. You don't try to become a child of God. You already are one. That's not the goal. That's the starting point. Once you know that — really know it, in your bones — now you can join what your Dad is doing. Not because you have to. Because it's who you are.

It's not about adding God to your life. It's about joining His work. It's not about earning His approval. It's about partnering with what He's already doing.

Stop auditioning for a role you've already been cast in.

You know LeBron James? Arguably the greatest basketball player to ever live. He's 40 now. Numbers are down. Not leading the league in scoring anymore.

But the Lakers are winning. And LeBron is thriving. He's playing a completely different role — defending, facilitating, being the connector. Doing the things that don't show up in the box score but absolutely help the team win.

Why? Because he already knows who he is. He doesn't need to score 40 every night to prove he's great. His identity isn't on trial. His legacy is secure. So he can play differently. He can sacrifice. He can do the unglamorous work.

That's what happens when you know you're a child of God. You don't have to earn His love every day. Your identity is secure. So now you can join what your Father is doing. You can serve from overflow instead of obligation. You can love people without keeping score.

"I have to" becomes "I get to." Obligation becomes invitation.

Remember the older brother? He served his father for years — and it felt like slavery. Because he never knew he was a son. He thought the work was the price of admission. But the father said, "Son, everything I have is already yours."

When you know you're a son, the same work feels completely different. It's not a burden. It's overflow. It's "I get to be part of what my Dad is doing."

That's not less obedience. That's deeper obedience. Obedience that flows from identity, not anxiety.

So let me ask you something. Are you trying to earn through performance what you already possess through identity?

Are you still on the porch trying to prove you belong when the Father has already opened the door and said "come in"? Are you still scoring to prove your worth when your name is already on the jersey? Are you still cleaning the house — trying to pay back a gift that was free?

What would change in your life this week if you actually believed — not just knew, but believed — that you are God's child and nothing you do can change that?

Sit with that for a second.

How would you show up at work differently? How would you treat the people in your house differently? How would you pray differently if you knew — really knew — that the one hearing your prayer is your Dad, not your boss?

And what if you started asking a different question this week? Not "What can I do for God?" — that's the orphan question. That's the performance question. What if you asked: "What is my Dad doing that I get to be part of?" Maybe it's the neighbor you keep meaning to invite over. Maybe it's the coworker who's going through something and you've been too busy to notice. What is your Dad doing — right around you — that you get to be part of?

Maybe God is asking you to see your whole week through that lens. Or maybe He's asking you to stop performing in one area — just stop — and find out that His love holds even when you're not producing.

Listen for what He's saying. He's your Father. He knows what you need.
