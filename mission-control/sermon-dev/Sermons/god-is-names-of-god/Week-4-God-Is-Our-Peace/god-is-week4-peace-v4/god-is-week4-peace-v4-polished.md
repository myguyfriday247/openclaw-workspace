# Week 4 Research Brief — God Is Our Peace

**Series:** God Is... Names of God
**Title:** God Is Our Peace
**Passage:** Judges 6:1–40
**Date:** June 7, 2026

---

## Big Idea

God reveals himself as **Yahweh-Shalom** in the middle of fear, guilt, oppression, and unfinished conflict. Peace in Judges 6 is not first the removal of trouble but the presence, word, and covenant mercy of God.

---

## Theological Burden

Judges 6 teaches that biblical peace runs deeper than emotional calm. Israel is impoverished, compromised, hiding, and under judgment. Gideon is fearful and hesitant. Yet into that chaos God speaks: **"Peace be to you. Do not fear; you shall not die"** (Judg. 6:23). The burden of this sermon is clear: **God is our peace because he reconciles fearful sinners to himself and steadies them before circumstances change.**

---

## Passage Overview (Judges 6:1–40)

### 1. Israel's Sin and Midianite Oppression (6:1–6)

Israel again does evil, and the Lord gives them into Midian's hand for seven years. Midianite raids devastate harvests, livestock, and ordinary life. Israel is driven into caves and mountain dens, reduced to hiding in the land God promised them.

**Key emphasis:** their loss of peace is covenantal before it is political.

### 2. The Prophet's Rebuke (6:7–10)

When Israel cries out, God does not immediately send a warrior. He sends a prophet. Before rescue comes explanation. The problem is not merely Midianite strength but Israel's disobedience — a people who have forgotten the God who delivered them from Egypt.

**Key emphasis:** peace requires truth about sin.

### 3. Gideon's Call (6:11–18)

Gideon is threshing wheat in a winepress — hiding from Midian, working in the wrong place out of sheer fear. The angel of the Lord addresses him as a "mighty man of valor" and promises divine presence. The title does not describe Gideon's current state; it names what God intends to make of him.

**Key emphasis:** God addresses Gideon by calling, not by observation. The name precedes the courage.

### 4. Divine Assurance and Yahweh-Shalom (6:19–24)

Gideon prepares an offering; fire from the rock consumes it; the angel vanishes. Gideon is gripped by terror — he has seen the angel of the Lord face to face and expects to die. But the Lord answers: **"Peace be to you. Do not fear; you shall not die."** In response, Gideon builds an altar and names it **Yahweh-Shalom** — "The Lord Is Peace."

**Key emphasis:** this is the theological center of the chapter — the moment where divine holiness and divine mercy meet.

### 5. Baal Must Fall (6:25–32)

That same night God commands Gideon to tear down his father's altar to Baal and cut down the Asherah pole. Peace is immediately linked to repentance and reform. The God who speaks peace will not share worship with idols.

**Key emphasis:** the God of peace is not tolerant of rival worship.

### 6. Spirit, Mobilization, and the Fleece (6:33–40)

The Spirit of the Lord clothes Gideon, armies gather, and yet Gideon still asks for confirming signs — twice. God patiently stoops to his weakness, accommodating doubt without endorsing it.

**Key emphasis:** peace does not erase trembling overnight.

## Word Studies

### Shalom (שָׁלוֹם)

**Meaning range:** peace, wholeness, soundness, welfare, safety, well-being, completeness.

In Scripture, *shalom* is never merely the absence of conflict. It includes right relationship with God, covenant well-being, inner and communal wholeness, and order under God's rule. The word carries the weight of the way things are supposed to be — life as God designed it to flourish.

In Judges 6, peace is spoken **before** Midian is defeated. This sequencing matters enormously, because it means peace here is:

- **objective before subjective** — grounded in God's word, not Gideon's feelings
- **relational before emotional** — restoring the divine-human bond before relieving anxiety
- **theological before therapeutic** — rooted in who God is before how Gideon feels

### Yahweh-Shalom (יְהוָה שָׁלוֹם)

This compound divine name occurs only in Judges 6:24. It means "The Lord Is Peace" — not merely that the Lord gives peace, but that he himself is its source and ground. Peace is found in God's own person, not in human strength or improved conditions.

The altar Gideon builds functions as memorial theology. It marks the place where terror was answered by divine mercy — a stone witness that the God who terrifies sinners by his holiness is the same God who speaks life to them.

## Historical / Cultural Context

### Midianite Raiding

Midian and her allies — Amalek and the people of the East — function as mobile raiders, likely striking during harvest season with devastating precision. Their goal is not permanent occupation but economic depletion: destroy the crops, scatter the livestock, and leave Israel too impoverished to resist.

This creates a grinding cycle of chronic insecurity, food scarcity, humiliation, and the loss of normal rhythms of life. Israel is not merely defeated; Israel is exhausted.

### Hiding in Caves

Israel was meant to dwell securely in the promised land, sitting under vine and fig tree without fear (cf. Mic. 4:4). Instead they live as fugitives in their own inheritance. The caves and dens are covenant shame made visible — the curses of Deuteronomy 28 in lived experience.

### Winepress Imagery

Threshing wheat normally happened on an open hilltop where wind could separate grain from chaff. A winepress is the wrong place — a low, enclosed space designed for crushing grapes, not winnowing grain. Gideon is working in concealment because fear has reshaped every ordinary activity.

**Preaching payoff:** God announces peace in a place of fear-shaped adaptation. He finds people where anxiety has driven them, not where courage has placed them.

### Household Idolatry

Baal and Asherah are not abstract national problems; they are in Gideon's own family compound. His father, Joash, maintains the altar. Before God deals with Midian on the outside, he deals with Baal on the inside. The sequence is theologically deliberate: worship must be purified before deliverance can proceed.

## Major Exegetical Insights

1. **The chapter begins with Israel's evil, not Midian's power.**
   The narrator interprets the crisis spiritually before presenting it militarily. Midian is the instrument; disobedience is the cause.

2. **God sends a prophet before a deliverer.**
   Repentance and interpretation must precede rescue. God will not save a people who do not yet understand why they needed saving.

3. **Gideon is called while afraid.**
   God's calling does not wait for human confidence. He names Gideon "mighty man of valor" while Gideon is crouching in a winepress.

4. **"The LORD is with you" is the foundation of everything.**
   Divine presence is the ground of both peace and courage. Without it, Gideon has neither.

5. **Judges 6:23–24 is the hinge of the chapter.**
   Yahweh-Shalom is born from God's word of life spoken to a man who expected death. The altar-name is the chapter's theological thesis.

6. **The altar is built before the battle is won.**
   Peace is confessed in unresolved circumstances. Gideon names God as peace while Midian still occupies the land.

7. **Baal's altar must be torn down.**
   Peace is moral and covenantal, not merely emotional. You cannot worship Baal at night and enjoy shalom by day.

8. **The fleece shows patient condescension.**
   God works with weak faith without endorsing unbelief. He accommodates Gideon's need for confirmation while still moving him toward obedience.

## Theological Themes

### 1. Peace After Guilt, Not Apart from It

The prophet's sermon in verses 7–10 makes clear that Israel's suffering is tied to covenant failure. God does not begin with comfort; he begins with diagnosis. Peace does not start with self-soothing but with God confronting rebellion — and then, remarkably, coming near anyway.

### 2. Peace in the Presence of Holiness

Gideon fears death because he has encountered the living God face to face. His deepest problem in that moment is not anxiety about Midian but terror before divine holiness. The peace God speaks addresses that deeper fear: *you have met me and you will not die.*

### 3. Peace Before Circumstances Change

Midian still occupies the land when Gideon names the altar Yahweh-Shalom. This is not naïve optimism; it is theological confession. God's peace can be named, trusted, and worshiped before the crisis is resolved — because its ground is God's character, not the situation's resolution.

### 4. Peace That Purifies

The God who speaks peace immediately orders idol demolition. Far from contradicting shalom, this confrontation enacts it. Biblical peace restores right worship, right order, and right allegiance. A peace that tolerates Baal is no peace at all.

### 5. Peace and Weakness

Gideon is not a natural hero. He is the youngest son of an obscure family, hiding in a winepress, full of questions. God's peace comes to weak people — and then steadies them for obedience. Strength is the fruit of peace, not its precondition.

## Christological Trajectory

Judges 6 should lead naturally and necessarily to Christ.

- Gideon fears death in God's presence; in Christ, believers have **peace with God** (Rom. 5:1).
- Gideon hears, "Peace be to you"; the risen Jesus greets his disciples with **"Peace I leave with you"** (John 14:27).
- Gideon builds an altar called Yahweh-Shalom; Paul declares of Jesus, **"He himself is our peace"** (Eph. 2:14).
- Gideon's peace is anticipatory and fragile; Christ's peace is accomplished through the cross and sealed by resurrection.

**Strong Christ line:**
What Gideon knew by altar-name, the church knows by incarnation, atonement, and resurrection: **the Lord is our peace because Christ himself is our peace.** The altar pointed forward; the cross fulfills.

## Objections and Pastoral Tensions

### Objection 1: "If God is our peace, why is Gideon still afraid?"

Because biblical peace is not the same thing as instant emotional composure. Gideon still trembles, still questions, still asks for signs. Peace begins in God's word and presence, then grows — often slowly — in lived experience. The altar is named Yahweh-Shalom before Gideon stops shaking.

### Objection 2: "Does this text promise that God will always change our circumstances quickly?"

No. Judges 6 shows peace announced before deliverance is complete. God gives himself first. The altar stands while the enemy still camps in the valley. Peace is real before resolution arrives.

### Objection 3: "Is peace here just inner calm?"

No. *Shalom* includes wholeness, covenant order, safety, well-being, and restored relationship with God. It is far richer than a feeling. Reducing it to inner calm loses the covenantal weight the word carries throughout Scripture.

### Objection 4: "Why does the God of peace command confrontation?"

Because biblical peace is not passive tolerance of evil. It is the restoration of right order under God's holy reign. Tearing down the altar to Baal is not a contradiction of shalom — it is a condition of it.

## Sermon Application

### For the anxious

God's peace is not built on your emotional steadiness. Gideon is shaky, uncertain, full of questions — and God still speaks peace over him. The ground of shalom is God's word, not your composure.

### For the ashamed

This chapter begins with Israel's guilt, yet the Lord still comes near. He does not wait for his people to clean themselves up. Peace is mercy extended to rebels.

### For the exhausted

Midianite oppression resembles chronic depletion — the slow erosion of hope through repeated loss. This text speaks directly to people who are worn down, not by a single crisis, but by the accumulation of many.

### For the hiding

God meets Gideon in concealment, in the wrong place, doing the wrong work out of fear. Hiddenness does not disqualify someone from divine calling. God walks into winepresses.

### For the idol-tolerant

You cannot protect Baal and enjoy Yahweh-Shalom. Peace requires reordered worship — and that reordering may cost something dear.

### For the church

Congregations often want the comfort of peace without the confrontation of repentance. Judges 6 refuses that division. The same God who speaks "peace be to you" commands "tear down the altar."

## Final Pastoral Synthesis

Judges 6 reveals Yahweh-Shalom in a chapter saturated with fear, compromise, poverty, and hesitation. That is exactly the point. God does not reveal himself as peace to people who already feel strong. He reveals himself as peace to the needy — to those who are hiding, compromised, and unable to save themselves. He speaks peace over trembling people, tears down the idols that fracture their allegiance, and begins the deliverance they could never accomplish alone.

In Christ, this peace is no longer only an altar-name carved in stone at Ophrah. It is embodied in a person, accomplished on a cross, vindicated in an empty tomb, and offered freely to the church. What Gideon confessed in hope, believers possess in full: **the Lord himself is our peace.**
