# Week 4 Research Brief — God Is Our Peace

**Series:** God Is... Names of God  
**Title:** God Is Our Peace  
**Passage:** Judges 6  
**Date:** June 7, 2026  

## Big Idea
God reveals himself as **Yahweh-Shalom** not by removing every threat immediately, but by meeting fearful, compromised, afflicted people with his presence, his word, and his saving purpose. In Judges 6, peace is not first a feeling; it is God's covenant assurance spoken into the middle of fear, guilt, and conflict.

## Main Theological Burden
This sermon should demonstrate that **peace in Scripture is deeper than calmness**. Israel is oppressed, guilty, hiding, and spiritually compromised. Gideon is fearful and hesitant. Yet into that chaos God speaks: **"Peace be to you. Do not fear; you shall not die"** (Judg. 6:23). The altar name **Yahweh-Shalom** declares that the Lord himself is the source of wholeness, safety, reconciliation, and rest — that peace begins with God's presence and word before a single circumstance has changed.

---

## 1) Passage Overview and Flow

### A. Israel's Sin and Midianite Oppression (6:1–6)
- Israel again "did what was evil in the sight of the LORD."
- God gives them into Midian's hand for seven years.
- Midian, Amalek, and the peoples of the east devastate crops and livestock.
- Israel hides in dens and caves.

**Preaching angle:** Their lack of peace is not random; it is covenantally connected to rebellion.

### B. God Sends a Prophet Before a Deliverer (6:7–10)
- Israel cries out.
- God first sends a prophet, not a military solution.
- The prophet reminds them of redemption from Egypt and confronts their disobedience.

**Preaching angle:** God interprets suffering before he removes it. Peace requires truth.

### C. Gideon's Call in Fear (6:11–18)
- Gideon threshes wheat in a winepress to hide from Midian.
- The angel of the LORD appears and calls him "mighty man of valor."
- Gideon questions God's presence and the absence of former wonders.

**Preaching angle:** God calls fearful people before they feel ready.

### D. Divine Revelation and Peace (6:19–24)
- Gideon prepares an offering.
- Fire consumes it.
- Gideon fears death after seeing the angel of the LORD.
- God says, "Peace be to you. Do not fear; you shall not die."
- Gideon builds an altar and names it **Yahweh-Shalom**.

**This is the theological center of the chapter.**

### E. Peace Requires the Tearing Down of Idols (6:25–32)
- Gideon must tear down his father's altar to Baal and cut down the Asherah.
- He obeys — though fearfully, by night.
- He is renamed Jerubbaal.

**Preaching angle:** God's peace is holy. It does not coexist peacefully with idols.

### F. Gideon's Weak Faith and the Fleece (6:33–40)
- War gathers.
- The Spirit clothes Gideon.
- Gideon still asks for signs.

**Preaching angle:** Peace does not mean instant maturity. God is patient with weak faith.

## 2) Key Word Studies

## Shalom (שָׁלוֹם)
**Meaning range:** peace, wholeness, completeness, welfare, safety, well-being, soundness, flourishing.

This distinction is crucial: in the Bible, **shalom is not merely the absence of conflict**. Its semantic field includes:
- right relationship with God
- covenant well-being
- personal and communal wholeness
- safety and stability
- ordered life under God's blessing

In Judges 6, shalom is spoken to a man who remains in external danger. Peace here is therefore not circumstantial ease but divine assurance and reconciliation — God's own word overruling what the situation would otherwise dictate.

## Yahweh-Shalom (יְהוָה שָׁלוֹם)
Occurs in Judges 6:24.

This altar name declares:
- **The LORD is peace**
- The LORD is the source and guarantor of peace
- Peace is grounded in God's character, not Israel's strength

The altar functions as a memorial: Gideon marks the place where terror was answered by divine word. The name does not describe a condition Gideon has achieved; it confesses a reality God has spoken into being.

## 3) Historical and Cultural Context

### Midianite Raiding Culture
The Midianites in Judges 6 appear as mobile raiders, not settled imperial occupiers. They invade at harvest, consume Israel's produce, devastate the agrarian economy, and leave God's people impoverished in the very land he gave them.

The cumulative effect is devastating:
- chronic insecurity rather than a single decisive defeat
- survival fear woven into the rhythm of daily life
- agricultural collapse that undermines communal stability
- deep humiliation for a people whose inheritance was meant to be marked by rest

### Hiding in Dens and Caves
This imagery communicates more than military weakness. Israel's covenant identity is collapsing. The people meant to dwell securely in God's promised land are now living like fugitives in their own territory.

### Winepress Instead of Threshing Floor
Threshing wheat in a winepress is abnormal — a small, enclosed space where the wind cannot separate chaff from grain. Gideon is adapting normal work to fearful conditions, doing what he can in a place designed for something else.

**Homiletical payoff:** Peace is announced in hiddenness, not triumph.

### Baal and Asherah in the Household
The family altar to Baal reveals a deeper issue beneath the surface crisis: Israel's fundamental problem is not only Midian on the outside but idolatry on the inside. Peace is therefore not simply deliverance from enemies; it requires repentance and the removal of rival allegiances.

## 4) Major Exegetical Insights

1. **The chapter begins with Israel's evil, not Midian's power.**  
   Scripture interprets trouble theologically before it addresses it strategically.

2. **God answers the cry with a prophet before a warrior.**  
   Repentance must precede restoration. God will not simply rescue people from their suffering without also addressing the sin that invited it.

3. **Gideon's fear is central to the narrative, not incidental to it.**  
   He is not introduced as bold but hiding — and that is precisely the person God calls.

4. **"The LORD is with you" (6:12) is the key promise.**  
   Divine presence is the foundation upon which every other dimension of peace is built.

5. **Verse 23 is the hinge of the chapter.**  
   "Peace be to you. Do not fear; you shall not die." Everything before it builds toward this word; everything after flows from it.

6. **The altar is built before the battle is won.**  
   Gideon confesses God as his peace while circumstances remain dangerous. Faith names what God has spoken, not what the eye can see.

7. **Peace is followed by idol demolition.**  
   The God who speaks peace also wages war against everything that destroys his people.

8. **The fleece shows God's patience with weak faith.**  
   Peace and trembling can coexist in a believer's experience. God does not revoke his word because Gideon is still shaking.

## 5) Theological Themes

## A. Peace as Reconciliation
Gideon fears death because he has encountered the holy God. The divine word of peace is therefore fundamentally a word of **mercy and preservation** — God absorbing the threat his own holiness poses to a sinful man.

## B. Peace in the Midst of Conflict
This passage does not say, "God is our peace because there are no enemies." It says God is peace **while Midian still exists**. The external threat has not been neutralized; the internal reality has been transformed.

## C. Peace and Holiness
God's peace is not sentimental niceness. It carries moral weight. For Gideon, receiving peace means Baal's altar must come down. God does not make peace with what is destroying his people.

## D. Peace and Divine Presence
The repeated promise throughout the chapter is not "you are enough," but **"I will be with you"** (6:16). Peace is tethered to presence, not to personality or circumstances.

## E. Peace and Weakness
God does not wait until Gideon becomes heroic. He reveals himself as peace to a man hiding in a winepress, and he patiently sustains that man through signs, trembling, and gradual obedience.

---

## 6) Christological Trajectory

Judges 6 should ultimately move toward Christ, where the altar-name finds its full embodiment.

- Gideon fears death in God's presence; in Christ we receive **peace with God** (Rom. 5:1).
- Gideon hears a divine word of peace; Jesus says, **"Peace I leave with you"** (John 14:27).
- Yahweh-Shalom finds its fullest revelation in **Ephesians 2:14: "He himself is our peace."**
- Christ does not merely announce peace; he accomplishes it through the cross, bearing the wrath that terrified Gideon and reconciling what Israel's repentance could never fully restore.

**Christ-centered line:**  
What Gideon knew by altar-name, the church knows by incarnation and cross: **the Lord is our peace because Christ himself is our peace.**

## 8) Objections and Pastoral Tensions

## Objection 1: "If God is our peace, why is Gideon still afraid?"
Because biblical peace is not identical to emotional steadiness. Gideon remains hesitant and trembling, yet God is still his peace. Peace begins in God's word and presence before it is fully realized in human experience. The altar-name confesses who God is, not how Gideon feels.

## Objection 2: "Does God's peace mean life gets easier?"
Not necessarily. Judges 6 shows that peace can be spoken before deliverance is complete. God often gives his people himself before he changes their situation. The gift precedes the resolution.

## Objection 3: "Is peace here just inner calm?"
No. Shalom is far more comprehensive. It includes:
- reconciliation with God
- covenant order
- moral restoration
- communal well-being
- eventual deliverance

Reducing peace to a feeling strips the word of its biblical weight.

## Objection 4: "Why would the God of peace call for tearing down altars and going to battle?"
Because biblical peace is not passive tolerance of evil. God restores peace by confronting what destroys his people. The tearing down of Baal's altar is not a contradiction of peace — it is a precondition for it.

## 9) Sermon Application

## For the anxious
God's peace is not built on your emotional control. Gideon is shaky from start to finish, yet God still speaks peace over him. The foundation is God's word, not your composure.

## For the ashamed
This chapter begins with guilt and compromise, yet God still comes near. Peace is mercy extended to sinners, not a reward for the righteous.

## For the exhausted
Israel is worn down by years of repeated loss and chronic depletion. This text speaks directly to people who are not facing a single crisis but living under sustained pressure.

## For those hiding
Gideon is found in concealment, doing diminished work in a fearful place. God does not wait for people to come out into the open. He meets them where they are.

## For idol-tolerant believers
You cannot cling to Baal and enjoy Yahweh's peace. Peace includes repentance — the willingness to let God dismantle what competes with him.

## For the church
Congregations may want comfort without reformation. Judges 6 insists that peace comes with truth and cleansing, not apart from them.

## 13) What Not to Do in This Sermon
- Don't reduce it to generic anxiety advice.
- Don't make the fleece the center of the text.
- Don't preach peace as mere positivity.
- Don't separate peace from repentance and holiness.
- Don't skip the corporate and covenantal dimensions of shalom.

---

## Final Pastoral Synthesis
Judges 6 introduces **Yahweh-Shalom** in a chapter saturated with fear, poverty, idolatry, and hesitation. That is precisely the point. God does not reveal himself as peace to people who already have themselves together. He reveals himself as peace to needy people who expect death and cannot save themselves. He speaks peace, demands repentance, and begins deliverance — all before the battle is fought. In Christ, this peace is no longer only an altar-name; it is embodied, purchased, and proclaimed. The church does not stand before a stone memorial and remember that God once spoke peace. It stands before the cross and knows that God has made peace — once for all, in his own blood.
