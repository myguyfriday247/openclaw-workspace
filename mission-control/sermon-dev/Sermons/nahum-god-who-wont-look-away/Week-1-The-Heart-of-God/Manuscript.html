🎙️ Week 1: The Heart of God

← SermonDev
Series: Nahum - "The God Who Won't Look Away" | Passage: Nahum Chapter 1 | Est. Length: 35-40 minutes

Have you ever watched the news and felt like the world is unraveling? Maybe it's the political division. Maybe it's the violence. Maybe it's something personal — a diagnosis, a betrayal, a loss that doesn't make sense. And you look at the chaos and you think, "Where is God in all of this?"

That's not a new question. It's the same question God's people have been asking for thousands of years.

Nahum was a prophet who lived in the southern kingdom of Judah around 600 BC. And he had a message for a people who were terrified. You see, Judah had lived under the shadow of the Assyrian Empire for decades. This was the most brutal superpower the world had ever seen. They had conquered nation after nation. They had destroyed cities. They had made victims of the Jews for generations.

And then — one day — everything changed. The Assyrian Empire collapsed. The empire that had seemed invincible... was gone.

That's what Nahum addresses. And what he says might surprise you.

Point 1: God Is Powerful — Creation Responds to His Authority

Look at how Nahum opens his prophecy. He describes a God before whom the sea dries up and the mountains quake. Not because He's flexing — but because His authority is absolute.

When He rebukes the sea — the primordial chaos — the waters retreat. When He speaks, rivers vanish, mountains tremble, hills melt. This isn't cosmic violence. It's cosmic order. Everything God made responds to His voice.

The same power that parted the Red Sea, stopped Jordan's flow, and brought down Jericho's walls is the power Nahum declares. We serve a God who doesn't negotiate with chaos. He commands it.

But here's what makes Nahum 1 remarkable: what God does with His power. He holds it. The same voice that could obliterate instead breathes. The same arm that could strike instead waits.

We've inherited a cultural imagination that equates power with violence, dominance with wrath. But Nahum shows us something different: absolute power choosing restraint. A God who could end everything choosing to sustain it. A God whose strength is measured not by what He destroys but by what He withholds.

And that restraint isn't weakness. It's mercy in action.

Illustration: The Leash on Chaos

There's a leash around chaos. You don't see it, but it's there. Every time there's order instead of entropy, every time the sun rises instead of the sky falling, every time gravity works instead of things floating away — that's God holding the leash. And He's choosing, right now, not to let go. Not because He can't. Because He loves.

Application

When we say God is powerful, we often think of spectacular displays — burning bushes, parting seas, collapsing walls. But Nahum's point is subtler: power that holds back is more remarkable than power that unleashes.

Think of the strongest person you know who never uses their strength to dominate. That's Nahum's God. He can — but He doesn't. Not because He can't. Because He won't.

And here's where this gets personal: how are you holding back power in your life? What strength are you not using because you know it would harm instead of help?

The gospel of God's power isn't "God can fix your problems." It's "God holds back the chaos that could consume you, and He does it every moment you're alive."

Action Steps
1. Identify one area where you're relying on your own strength instead of trusting God's power. Write it down. Then pray: "God, I trust You to handle this in Your way and timing."
2. Practice restraint today. Find one situation where you could unleash force (verbal, emotional, physical) but choose instead to hold back. Notice how that feels.
3. Thank God for His restraint. Before bed, thank Him for three things He's held back in your life — circumstances, consequences, or chaos that could have consumed you but didn't.

Point 2: God Is Just (and Patient) — His Anger Is Measured, Not Reckless

The Hebrew phrase is striking: "slow to anger" literally reads "long of nostrils." The image is Someone breathing deeply before responding. Picture someone whose nostrils flare in righteous displeasure — but instead of lashing out, they breathe. Deeply. They deliberate. They choose.

This isn't indifference. It's restraint.

And here's why this matters: we serve a God who feels anger genuinely. His wrath isn't performed or artificial. But His anger is always measured, always purposeful, never reckless. Unlike our responses — quick, wounding, regretful — God's anger calculates, considers, and acts only when justice demands it.

The Hebrew word for this patience appears ten times in the Old Testament. It's not a passive quality. It's an active decision. Every time God withholds judgment, He is choosing restraint. Every day that evil persists, God is breathing through the injustice.

This doesn't mean God approves of sin. It means He is giving time. Time for repentance. Time for rescue. Time for His purposes to unfold.

And here's the comfort for Judah — and for us: when Nahum announces God's "long of nostrils," he's announcing hope. The Assyrians may be fierce, but their fury is matched by a God who is slow to anger. Not because He's soft. Because He's sovereign. He will act — but on His timeline, in His way, for His glory and His people's good.

Illustration: The Deep Breath

You know that feeling when someone pushes your button and you feel your blood rise? Your nostrils flare. Your jaw tightens. Now imagine choosing to breathe instead of strike. Counting to ten. Deliberately.

That's what "long of nostrils" looks like. God feels the same righteous anger — but He breathes. Deeply. And in that breath, there's room for mercy, space for repentance, time for rescue.

Application

"God is patient" isn't a theological abstraction. It's the oxygen of your spiritual life. Every morning you wake up, every breath you take, every opportunity you have to change — that's God's patience in action.

He's not ignoring your sin. He's not pretending it doesn't matter. He's giving you time. Time to repent. Time to turn. Time to trust.

And here's the uncomfortable application: how patient are you with others? When someone wrongs you, do you breathe deeply — or explode?

God's patience with you should produce patience in you. Not because you earn His patience by being patient — but because you've experienced it. You know what it feels like to be given time you don't deserve. Now give that to someone else.

Action Steps
1. Identify one person who frustrates you. Instead of venting or fuming, breathe deeply (literally — count to five) and pray for them by name. Do this every time you feel frustration this week.
2. Ask God to show you where you're quick to anger. Is there a pattern in your responses? A trigger? Ask the Holy Spirit to reveal it, then write it down.
3. Practice the "24-hour rule." When someone wrongs you, wait 24 hours before responding. Use that time to process through prayer, not social media venting.

Point 3: God Is Merciful — Good News for Judah (and for Us)

Right in the middle of Nahum's oracle of judgment comes a sudden pivot: "The LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble; and He knows those who trust in Him."

After eight verses of cosmic power and righteous wrath, we get this. Goodness. Mercy.

Not because God changed His mind about Nineveh — the judgment still comes — but because Judah needed to know: God's wrath isn't undiscriminating. His justice isn't blind. He sees who belongs to Him. He knows those who trust in Him. And for them, He is a stronghold. A fortress. A refuge when everything else falls apart.

This is the gospel arc in miniature. The same God who devastates Nineveh is the God who comforts Judah. The same fist that crushes evil is the open hand that rescues the faithful.

Nahum isn't presenting a split personality. He's presenting the fullness of God's character. Justice and mercy aren't opposites in God's nature — they're the same attribute viewed from different angles. His justice demands sin be addressed. His mercy provides a way through.

For Judah, that meant liberation from Assyrian bondage. For us, it means something greater: a God who knows us, protects us, and holds wrath while offering refuge.

The question isn't whether God's wrath is real. The question is whether we're among those He knows.

Illustration: The Mother Bird

Hawks circle overhead. The mother bird doesn't flee. She spreads her wings, covers her babies, refuses to move. The hawks could take her. She's not stronger than them. But she doesn't care about herself — only about protecting what's hers.

That's mercy. That's God. Not distant, not indifferent — present, protective, fierce in His love.

Application

When Nahum says God "knows those who trust in Him," he's not talking about information. He's talking about relationship. God knows you the way a shepherd knows His sheep. Not abstractly, but intimately. By name. By history. By the number of hairs on your head and the depth of your struggles.

And here's the mercy in this: you don't have to perform for God. You don't have to earn His attention. If you trust Him, He knows you.

That doesn't mean life will be easy — Judah still faced Assyria. But it means you don't face it alone. You have a stronghold. A refuge. Someone who sees, knows, and protects.

And here's the application: how are you running to other strongholds instead of the One who actually protects?

Action Steps
1. Write down three things you're relying on for security (job, relationships, savings, health). Then ask: "If this were taken away tomorrow, would I still trust God as my stronghold?"
2. Thank God for knowing you personally. Before bed, thank Him that He knows your name, your struggles, your failures, and still calls you His.
3. Choose one area where you're trying to protect yourself (control, anxiety, planning) and intentionally release it to God in prayer this week.

Point 4: God Is Angry (at Sin, Not People) — His Wrath Protects

When Nahum pronounces God's jealousy and vengeance, we flinch. We've absorbed a gospel of safety, where God's love is soft and His wrath is hidden. But Nahum doesn't let us hide.

God's anger is real. His jealousy is fierce.

And here's what we need to understand: this anger isn't temper. It's protective love. The Hebrew word for "jealous" appears in the Ten Commandments — "the LORD your God is a jealous God." This isn't petty envy. It's the fierce love of a husband for His bride, a father for His children. When God is angry, He's not losing control. He's loving fiercely.

And here's the uncomfortable truth this side of the cross: God's anger is always against sin, never against people as such. Even Nineveh's destruction was aimed at the evil the empire embodied. The people? God had already shown mercy through Jonah — 150 years before.

What Nineveh faced wasn't God's arbitrary rage but the accumulated weight of unrepented violence, injustice, and oppression. When God judges, He's not attacking people. He's ending systems of death. He's clearing space for life.

And for us, this means something profound: the same fierce love that will ultimately destroy everything that kills and steals and destroys is the love that holds us now.

God's wrath isn't our enemy. It's our rescue.

Illustration: The Movie "Taken"

You remember the movie "Taken." Bryan Mills, former CIA, tells the kidnappers: "I don't have money. But what I do have is a very particular set of skills. Skills I've acquired over a very long career. Skills that make me a nightmare for people like you."

He's not an unhinged murderer. He's a father protecting his daughter. When he tracks down the traffickers, there's not a person in that theater who wants him to show restraint. We cheer every blow. We celebrate every life taken.

Because it's protection. Because it's love.

That's God's wrath. Not cosmic tantrum. Fierce, protective, righteous love.

Application

Here's the paradox Nahum presents: the same God who wrathfully destroys is the same God who mercifully protects. And both are expressions of love.

A father who lets his child run into traffic because he "doesn't want to be controlling" isn't loving. He's failing. But a father who disciplines, protects, and yes, gets angry at what harms his child — that's love.

And here's where this cuts: what in your life are you defending as "not that bad" while God sees it as death? What habit, relationship, attitude, or addiction are you minimizing while it slowly kills you?

God's wrath isn't your enemy. It's His love fighting for your life.

The question isn't whether God's angry. The question is whether you'll receive His protection instead of resisting it.

Action Steps
1. Examine one area of your life where you've minimized sin. Ask the Holy Spirit: "Is this something You've been wanting to address in me?" Listen. Don't deflect.
2. Thank God for His protective anger. Thank Him that He's not indifferent to the evil in the world — that He will ultimately destroy everything that steals and kills.
3. Identify one "small" sin you've been tolerating. Confess it. Then choose one concrete action to turn from it this week — not to earn God's love, but because you trust His protection.

Conclusion

The prophet's voice echoes across twenty-seven centuries, but the question lands in your lap today:

Who is your God?

Is He a cosmic killjoy, keeping score, waiting for you to mess up? Or is He a Father who holds back the chaos that could consume you — not because He can't, but because He loves?

Nahum shows us both. God's anger is real. His wrath is fierce. But His patience is longer than you can imagine. His mercy is deeper than you can fathom. His knowledge of you is more intimate than you can grasp.

And here's the gospel:

The same God who unleashed judgment on Nineveh — who avenged His people, who made "an utter end" of the place thereof — that same God sent His Son to absorb the judgment we deserved.

The cross is where wrath and mercy meet. Where justice and love collide. Where God dealt with sin without dealing with sinners.

You don't have to fear God's anger. You don't have to earn His patience. You don't have to perform for His attention.

You just have to trust.

Point of Decision

If God's anger is patient, purposeful, and rooted in love — what will you do with that?

  1. Stop running from the God who already paid your debt.
    You can't out-sin His grace. You can't out-wait His patience. He's not chasing you down to punish you. He's pursuing you to love you.
  2. Trust His timing.
    When life feels chaotic, when evil seems to win, when you wonder if God sees — remember: He's holding the leash. He's breathing through the injustice. He's working on a timeline you can't see.
  3. Rest in the finished work of Christ.
    The cross isn't God reluctantly saving you. It's God passionately rescuing you. He didn't hold back. He didn't breathe and wait. He struck once — and it was enough.

The God who won't look away? He's not looking at you with furrowed brow. He's looking at you with arms wide open.

The question isn't whether He'll accept you.
The question is whether you'll let Him.

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for being a God of power who holds back chaos. Thank You for Your patience — for breathing through our sin, giving us time to repent. Thank You for Your mercy — for knowing us, protecting us, calling us Yours. Thank You for Your anger — for loving us fiercely enough to protect us from what would destroy us.

But thank You most of all for Jesus. Thank You that He took the judgment we deserve. Thank You that on the cross, Your wrath and Your mercy met. Thank You that because of His sacrifice, we can come to You not as criminals to a judge, but as children to a Father.

For anyone here today who has been running — stop. You don't have to run anymore. The debt is paid. The leash is in hands that love you. The breath is being held for you, not against you.

We love You, Lord. We trust You. We surrender to You.

In Jesus' name, amen.
