Week 3: The Gospel Is Your Only Hope to Change

Series: We Hold These Truths | Week: 3 of 4

Key Passage: Romans 1:16

INTRODUCTION & BURDEN

Every person secretly wants to change. They want to be better spouses, parents, friends. They want to overcome their addictions, their anger, their anxiety. They've tried everything—self-help books, therapy, meditation, willpower, New Year's resolutions—and nothing seems to stick.

That's because every system of change except one is fundamentally broken. They all rely on human effort to produce divine transformation. But Romans 1:16 declares something radical: "For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes."

The burden: To show people that the gospel isn't just the door to heaven—it's the power for daily transformation. The gospel isn't plan B after everything else fails. It's the only hope for real, lasting change.

POINT 1: EVERY PERSON WANTS TO CHANGE

1.1 The Universal Desire

Deep down, every human being wants to change. This is observable across all cultures, ages, and backgrounds. Consider:

  • The $30+ billion self-help industry—people spend billions trying to improve themselves (Industry data, Statista 2024)
  • New Year's resolutions—over 40% of Americans make them, showing the universal desire for self-improvement (Gallup, 2023)
  • Therapy and counseling—millions seek professional help for change
  • Quiet attempts—most people are privately fighting battles no one sees
"The human heart is a factory of desires—endless wants, endless striving, endless disappointment." — John Piper
John Piper

1.2 The Problem of Wanting Change But Not Changing

There's a gap between what we want to become and what we actually become. Romans 7:15-19 captures this beautifully:

"For I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate... For I know that nothing good dwells in me, that is, in my flesh. For I have the desire to do what is right, but not the ability to carry it out." — Romans 7:15-19

This is the human condition. We want to change, but we're trapped. Our willpower fails. Our best intentions crumble.

1.3 The Deeper Problem: It's Not Just Behavior

Most approaches to change treat the symptom, not the disease. They try to modify behavior without addressing the heart. But:

  • You can change your actions but not your desires
  • You can suppress sin but not kill it
  • You can perform but not truly transform

The heart is the issue— Proverbs 4:23 says "Above all else, guard your heart, for everything you do flows from it."

POINT 2: WHY EVERY OTHER SYSTEM FAILS

2.1 The Five Failed Systems of Change

1. Willpower / Self-Effort

The promise: If you try hard enough, you can change.

The problem: Romans 7 shows we don't have the power to do what we know is right. Willpower is like trying to lift yourself up by your own bootstraps.

2. Self-Help / Positive Thinking

The promise: Believe in yourself and you can achieve anything.

The problem: Self-esteem is built on shifting sand. The Bible says the heart is deceitful above all things (Jeremiah 17:9). Believing in yourself when you're the problem is circular reasoning.

3. Religion / Rule-Keeping

The promise: If you follow the rules, you'll be acceptable to God and others.

The problem: Religion addresses behavior, not the heart. It creates outward conformity but inward chaos. Jesus criticized the Pharisees for exactly this—they looked righteous on the outside but were full of greed and self-indulgence (Matthew 23:25-28).

4. Therapy / Psychology

The promise: Understanding your past and processing your wounds will lead to healing.

The value: There's genuine help here. God can use wise counsel. But ultimately, psychology describes the problem rather than providing the solution. It can help you cope with sin's consequences but cannot defeat sin itself.

5. Moral Community / Accountability

The promise: Surround yourself with good people and you'll become better.

The problem: This can help maintain behavior but cannot transform the heart. You can hang out with the best people and still be a mess inside.

2.2 Why They All Fail: The Fundamental Issue

The Core Problem

Every system of change (except the gospel) assumes:

The same power that created the problem can solve it.

But sin isn't just bad behavior—it's a condition of the heart. You can't use human power to transform a human heart. That's like trying to use a dirty rag to clean a dirty rag. You need something from outside—divine power.

Source: Adapted from C.J. Mahaney, "The Cross Centered Life"

"Religion is behavior modification—Christianity is heart transformation." — Unknown

2.3 The Religion vs. Gospel Distinction

Religion Says... The Gospel Says...
"Try harder to keep God's rules" "Christ has kept God's rules for you"
"Your salvation depends on your performance" "Your salvation depends on Christ's performance"
"Change happens by working on yourself" "Change happens by receiving what Christ did for you"
"You must become good enough for God" "God already accepts you through Christ"
"Behavior modification is the goal" "Heart transformation is the goal"

POINT 3: WHAT MAKES THE GOSPEL DIFFERENT

3.1 The Gospel Addresses the Heart, Not Just Behavior

Every other system tries to fix the outside. The gospel transforms the inside.

"Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, the new creation has come: The old has gone, the new is here!" — 2 Corinthians 5:17

This isn't behavior modification. This is new creation. The Greek word for "new creation" (ktisis) implies something utterly new—not renovated, not improved, but created afresh. This is the same word used in Genesis 1:1 for God's original creation. It's not "reformation" but "re-creation."

Source: Greek grammar notes, cf. BDAG lexicon

3.2 The Gospel Provides Power, Not Just Instructions

Religion gives you rules to follow. The gospel gives you power to change.

"For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes." — Romans 1:16

The Greek word for "power" is dunamis—the same root word from which we get "dynamite." This isn't passive power like having electricity available; this is active, explosive power that accomplishes what it promises. The gospel isn't just informative—it's transformative. It doesn't just tell you what to do—it does what it demands.

Source: Greek grammar notes, cf. BDAG lexicon

3.3 The Gospel Offers Righteousness, Not Just Improvement

Every other system says "become righteous." The gospel says "receive righteousness."

"For in the gospel the righteousness of God is revealed—a righteousness that is by faith from first to last, just as it is written: 'The righteous will live by faith.'" — Romans 1:17

This was Martin Luther's breakthrough that sparked the Reformation. We don't earn righteousness—we receive it. And when we receive it, we are transformed by it.

3.4 The Gospel Provides the Holy Spirit, Not Just Motivation

The gospel doesn't just motivate us to change—it gives us the Holy Spirit who actually changes us.

"But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control..." — Galatians 5:22-23

Notice: it's the fruit of the Spirit, not the fruit of our effort. These qualities grow naturally when the Spirit is at work in our hearts.

"The gospel is not a recipe for Christian living—it is the power for Christian living." — Mark Dever
Mark Dever

POINT 4: THE GOSPEL IS THE POWER OF GOD

4.1 What "Power of God" Means

When Paul says the gospel is "the power of God," he means:

  • It's God's power, not human power—we don't bring the power; we receive it
  • It's salvation power—not just from hell, but from sin's power
  • It's for everyone who believes—this power is available to all

4.2 The Three Dimensions of Salvation

When the Bible talks about salvation, it includes three dimensions (often called the "already/not yet" tension):

Past
Justification
Saved from the penalty of sin (Romans 3:24-26)
Present
Sanctification
Saved from the power of sin (1 Thessalonians 4:3)
Future
Glorification
Saved from the presence of sin (Romans 8:30)

The gospel saves us in all three tenses. It's not just "fire insurance"—it's power for daily living. Justification is a one-time event (positional change), sanctification is a progressive process (growth in holiness), and glorification is our future hope (complete redemption).

Source: Systematic theology summaries (e.g., Grudem, Wayne)

4.3 The Gospel Changes Desires, Not Just Actions

This is the key difference. The gospel doesn't just tell you what to do—it gives you a new heart that wants to do it.

"I will give you a new heart, and I will put a new spirit in you. I will remove your stony heart from your body and replace it with a living heart." — Ezekiel 36:26

This is why the gospel is the only hope. It doesn't just modify behavior—it transforms desires. It doesn't just clean the outside—it renews the inside.

4.4 The Gospel Produces Lasting Change

Every other system produces temporary change. The gospel produces eternal change.

  • Behavior modification is fragile—circumstances can undo it
  • Heart transformation is durable—the new nature persists
  • This is why Christians can endure suffering, persecution, and trials
"The power that raised Christ from the dead is the same power working in us." — Romans 8:11 (paraphrased)

ILLUSTRATIONS

1. The Car Engine Analogy

Behavior modification is like trying to drive a car by pushing it from the outside. The gospel is like putting a new engine in the car. You don't just try harder to move forward—you have actual power to move.

2. The Cosmetic vs. Cosmetic Surgery Analogy

Behavior modification is cosmetic—it's just surface change. The gospel is cosmetic surgery—it's deep transformation that changes your whole appearance.

3. The Dead Man Walking Analogy

A dead man can try as hard as he wants to walk, but he can't do it. He's dead. But if you give him life, walking comes naturally. We're spiritually dead in our sins (Ephesians 2:1)—that's why we can't change ourselves. We need life from God.

4. The iPhone Update Analogy

Behavior modification is like changing your phone's wallpaper—superficial. The gospel is like a software update that changes how the phone actually works. It's a complete overhaul.

5. Martin Luther's Story

Martin Luther was a monk trying desperately to be good enough for God. He fasted, prayed, performed every religious duty—yet had no peace. Then he discovered Romans 1:17—"The righteous will live by faith." He realized he couldn't earn righteousness; he could only receive it. This breakthrough changed not only his life but the course of history.

    • OBJECTION HANDLING

    "I've tried being a Christian and didn't change."

    Response: Were you trusting in your own effort or in the power of the gospel? There's a difference between "Christianity as religion" and "Christianity as faith." If you've been trying to change by your own willpower, that's religion. The gospel is about receiving Christ's power through faith.

    "Doesn't the Bible say we have to work hard and be disciplined?"

    Response: Yes, but discipline flows from transformation, not the other way around. The Spirit produces the fruit; we don't produce it ourselves. We're called to "work out your salvation with fear and trembling" (Philippians 2:12), but that salvation was "worked out" for us (2:13)—the power comes from God.

    "What about therapy and counseling? Is that wrong?"

    Response: Not at all. God can use wise counsel. But the ultimate answer isn't understanding your past—it's the power of the gospel. Therapy can help you cope with the effects of sin; the gospel defeats sin itself. Use both wisely.

    "This sounds too easy. Doesn't real change require hard work?"

    Response: It's not easy in the sense that it's simple to understand. But it's powerful. And it's not "easy" in the sense that it doesn't require anything from us—it requires everything: trust, faith, dependence. That's hard for proud humans. But the change it produces is deep and lasting.
